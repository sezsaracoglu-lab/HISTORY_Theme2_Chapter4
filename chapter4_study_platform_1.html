IV. Behaviourism & Cognition

Chapter 4 · Study Platform

How do you explain and control behaviour?

The rise of behaviourism and its replacement by the cognitive paradigm — a story of three rejections, two paradigms, and one stubborn question that psychology has never stopped asking.
§ 0 · The Big Picture

What this chapter is actually about

Three orientation points before we go in.

Central Question

Can psychology be a science of behaviour without invoking the mind?

Behaviourism's answer was yes — for fifty years, mental content was banished from psychology. Cognitive psychology's answer was: only partly, and not for long.

Why it matters

Scientific status, real-world reach, and the methodology of mind

Behaviourism converted public opinion to the view that psychology is a science. It also produced enduringly useful tools — reinforcement, conditioning, applied behaviour analysis — that still shape advertising, education, and clinical practice. The cognitive paradigm then re-opened the question of how mental content can itself be studied scientifically.

Field connection

Two metatheoretical assumptions, two paradigms, one continuous story

Paradigms in psychology rest on untested metatheoretical assumptions. Behaviourism assumed humans are like animals and behaviour is controlled by its consequences. Cognitive psychology kept some of that and added information-processing — building on rather than replacing what came before.

Reading lens

Behaviourism declined not because it was proved wrong, but because it failed to explain behaviours that exist in humans but not in rats. The cognitive paradigm asked a different kind of question — and that is what changed psychology.

01
Branch One

The Failure of Introspection

Behaviourism did not appear from nowhere. It arose as an explicit rejection of the introspectionist paradigm — a rejection grounded in evidence, not prejudice. This branch covers the two evidential failures: introspection couldn't explain certain higher mental processes, and different laboratories produced different results from the same method.

We begin where the chapter begins — with two problems Wundt's students discovered, in his own tradition, that could not be explained by looking inward. Each concept that follows tightens the case against introspection.

01Introspection
Definition

A method in which trained observers report the contents of their own consciousness in order to study mental structure or function.

Explanation

Used by Wundt to study the structure of the mind and by James and the functionalists to study its function. Behaviourism arose specifically as the rejection of this method because it failed to deliver replicable, scientific findings about behaviour.

Example

A trained observer is asked to lift two weights and report on the residual mental sensation that allows them to compare which is heavier — Marbe's classic 1901 design.

Exam application

"Why was introspection rejected as a method by experimental psychologists?"

Model answer: Two evidential reasons. First, introspection failed to explain certain phenomena — Marbe's weight-judgement, Watt's object-naming, Ach's determining tendency all showed behaviour occurring without conscious mental content. Second, different laboratories produced different results using the method (the imageless thought controversy), so introspection failed the criterion of replicability that defines a science.

02The Würzburg School
Definition

A research group founded by Oswald Külpe (1862–1915), who had been Wundt's student, that used introspection to study higher mental processes — which Wundt himself had ruled outside experimental psychology.

Explanation

The Würzburg studies are pivotal because they exposed the limits of the very method they used. By systematically asking introspectors to report on judgement, naming and willing, the school produced the evidence that introspection could not explain higher mental processes — building on the previous concept's definition.

Example

Marbe (1901) on weight judgement, Watt (1913) on naming, and Ach (1910) on the will were all Würzburg studies.

Exam application

"Identify the Würzburg school and explain its significance for the history of behaviourism."

Model answer: Founded by Külpe, the school broke with Wundt's prohibition on studying higher mental processes experimentally. Its significance is paradoxical: it set out to extend introspection but ended up generating the evidence — Marbe, Watt, Ach — that demonstrated introspection's failure, paving the way for behaviourism.

03Marbe's Weight-Judgement Study (1901)
Definition

An experiment in which participants lifted two weights in succession and reported which was heavier. Marbe expected a residual mental sensation of the first weight; he did not find one.

Explanation

This single negative result — that people simply could not introspect the basis of their judgement — is foundational. It anticipates Kahneman's modern finding that the reasons we report for our actions are not necessarily the reasons that direct our behaviour.

Example

Lift weight A, lift weight B, say which is heavier. Most people can do it. Almost no one can say how they did it.

Exam application

"Briefly describe Marbe's 1901 study and explain its theoretical significance."

Model answer: Marbe asked participants to compare two lifted weights and introspect the basis of their judgement. He expected a residual mental sensation of the first weight but found none. The result mattered because it suggested that introspection is not a useful tool for finding out how the mind causes behaviour — a finding later cognitive psychologists, including Kahneman, would corroborate.

04Watt's Object-Naming Studies (1913)
Definition

H. J. Watt's investigation of how people name objects, which found that the name is produced without conscious thought — it just appears in consciousness from nowhere.

Explanation

A second blow to introspection from the same school. Where Marbe showed judgement is opaque to introspection, Watt showed naming is too. The pattern is now visible: higher cognitive acts proceed without introspectible content.

Example

Show someone a chair. They say "chair" instantly. Ask them how they did it. They cannot tell you anything beyond "I just knew."

Exam application

"What did Watt's research add to the case against introspection?"

Model answer: Watt extended Marbe's argument from judgement to naming. He found that object names appear in consciousness without conscious thought, confirming that introspection cannot illuminate the cognitive processes that generate everyday behaviour — even something as basic as labelling an object.

05Ach's Determining Tendency
Definition

A tendency to achieve a particular goal where there is no conscious awareness of the intention at the moment the action is carried out.

Explanation

Ach (1871–1946) studied what was then called "the will" and is now called motivation. He showed that goal-directed behaviour can occur without prior conscious thought — completing the trio of evidence (with Marbe and Watt) that the introspectionist programme could not handle.

Example

You intend to go to the shops but find yourself walking home instead. Ach's number-task version: shown "6 and 2" preceded by the cued word "add", participants invariably said "8" — without awareness that the word had directed the response.

Exam application

"What is a determining tendency, and why is it problematic for introspection?"

Model answer: A determining tendency is goal-directed behaviour produced without conscious awareness of the relevant intention. It is problematic for introspection because it shows that some behaviour is shaped by mental influences that are simply not available to introspective report — undermining introspection's claim to be a complete method.

06The Imageless Thought Controversy
Definition

A dispute over whether thought without images is possible. Bühler and Woodworth said yes; Wundt and Titchener said no.

Explanation

This is the second major problem for introspection. Where the Würzburg studies showed introspection couldn't explain certain phenomena, the imageless-thought dispute showed introspection couldn't even agree with itself across laboratories. Each side accused the other's introspectors of being insufficiently trained or of suggestion-induced illusion.

Example

Bühler's lab reports thought without images; Titchener's lab — using the extensive training Titchener insisted upon — reports the opposite. The controversy was never resolved.

Exam application

"Why does the imageless thought controversy matter for the scientific status of psychology?"

Model answer: If different laboratories produce different results using the same method, that method cannot be described as scientific — replicability is a defining criterion of a science. The controversy was never resolved, which provided fertile ground for a new psychology, behaviourism, that rejected introspection altogether.

07Replicability as a Criterion of Science
Definition

The principle that the same method, applied competently in different laboratories, should yield the same findings.

Explanation

Where Marbe, Watt and Ach attacked introspection's scope (it can't explain everything), the imageless-thought controversy attacked its reliability. Together these failures made introspection scientifically untenable and prepared the ground for Watson's rejection.

Example

Two physics labs measuring the speed of light should agree. Two psychology labs using introspection on imageless thought did not.

Exam application

"How does the criterion of replicability bear on the rejection of introspection?"

Model answer: Replicability is a defining criterion of science. Introspection failed it: the imageless-thought dispute showed that competent labs reached opposing conclusions using the same method. This methodological failure — independent of any worry about scope — was sufficient on its own to push psychology toward methodological alternatives such as behaviourism.

02
Branch Two

The Foundations of Behaviourism

Even before "behaviourism" had a name, two researchers were already laying its substrate: Thorndike with cats in puzzle boxes, Pavlov with dogs and bells. Their laws and conditioning principles gave Watson the empirical material for a new psychology.

If Branch 1 explains why a new psychology was needed, Branch 2 explains where its raw materials came from. Each concept here builds the case that animal behaviour can be lawfully described — without recourse to introspection.

08Metatheoretical Assumptions of Behaviourism
Definition

Untested foundational assumptions on which the paradigm rests: (a) the mechanisms that cause behaviour are the same for rats and humans, and (b) behaviour should be explained in a particular way (without reference to mental content).

Explanation

A paradigm is not just a collection of theories but a set of background commitments. Behaviourism's two commitments — animal-human continuity and a specific explanatory format — define what counts as a behaviourist explanation regardless of the specific theorist.

Example

Watson studying rat behaviour in order to understand human behaviour assumes (a). Skinner refusing to invoke internal states assumes (b).

Exam application

"What are the two main metatheoretical assumptions of behaviourism?"

Model answer: First, that human behaviour can be predicted from observations of animal behaviour — there is no fundamental dividing line between animals and humans. Second, that behaviour is controlled by its consequences and should be explained in a particular form, without recourse to mental content.

09Thorndike's Puzzle Box
Definition

An apparatus in which a hungry cat must perform a particular action to escape and reach food outside. Time-to-escape was measured across repeated trials.

Explanation

Thorndike (1874–1949) ran his experiments in William James's basement at Harvard because psychology departments did not yet study animals. The gradual decrease in escape time across trials was the empirical foundation for his two laws.

Example

A kitten placed in a box with food outside takes 200 seconds to escape on trial 1; by trial 20, only 8 seconds. Learning had occurred — without introspection.

Exam application

"Describe Thorndike's puzzle box experiments and explain their importance."

Model answer: Thorndike placed hungry cats in puzzle boxes requiring different solutions and timed their escape over repeated trials. Time-to-escape decreased gradually, suggesting incremental learning. The experiments mattered because they showed that systematic, lawful behaviour change could be studied without invoking mental content — providing the empirical basis for the law of effect.

10Thorndike's Law of Effect
Definition

"Of several responses made to the same situation, those which are accompanied or closely followed by satisfaction will be more firmly connected with the situation… those which are accompanied or closely followed by discomfort will have their connections weakened" (Thorndike, 1911, p. 244).

Explanation

In plain language: animals do more of what they enjoy and less of what they don't. The novelty was not the idea but the quasi-scientific framing as a "law" — psychology, like physics, now had laws. The concept of "satisfaction" was, however, defined circularly (satisfaction = whatever increased response probability).

Example

The cat that brushes against the latch and escapes does so faster on subsequent trials. The brushing was followed by satisfaction (food, freedom), so the connection is strengthened.

Exam application

"State Thorndike's law of effect and identify one limitation."

Model answer: Responses followed by satisfaction become more strongly connected to the situation; responses followed by discomfort become less so. The principal limitation is that "satisfaction" is defined circularly — it is identified by its effect on behaviour rather than independently. Skinner later avoided this by simply identifying the conditions that were reinforcing.

11Thorndike's Law of Exercise
Definition

"Any response to a situation will be more strongly connected with the situation in proportion to the number of times it has been connected with that situation and to the average vigor and duration of the connections" (Thorndike, 1911, p. 244).

Explanation

Animals tend to repeat behaviours they have done before. The idea was already present in James's analysis of habit; Thorndike's contribution was the formal statement. Sits alongside the law of effect as the empirical groundwork Watson would inherit.

Example

A rat that has run a maze 50 times runs it more reliably than a rat that has run it twice — independently of any reward.

Exam application

"How do the law of effect and the law of exercise differ?"

Model answer: The law of effect concerns the consequences of a response (satisfaction strengthens, discomfort weakens). The law of exercise concerns the frequency, vigour and duration of the response itself. They were intended to operate jointly: practice strengthens the connection, and outcomes determine which connections are practiced.

12Reactive Inhibition & Spontaneous Alternation
Definition

Reactive inhibition: a build-up of resistance to repeating an action. Spontaneous alternation: the observed phenomenon that a rat reinforced for turning right in a T-maze will, after some trials, turn left.

Explanation

This is the gentle counter-example to Thorndike's laws. Despite reinforcement always supporting one response, the animal alternates. The textbook frames this as "the gods punishing hubris" — psychological laws are messier than physical laws.

Example

You have been revising for two hours. Your boredom is reactive inhibition. The remedy: short break, do something different.

Exam application

"What is spontaneous alternation, and what does it suggest about Thorndike's laws?"

Model answer: A rat consistently reinforced for turning right will eventually turn left — spontaneous alternation. This contradicts the strict prediction of the law of effect and is explained by reactive inhibition, which accumulates with repetition and dissipates over time. It demonstrates that Thorndike's laws are useful approximations rather than exceptionless laws of behaviour.

13Pavlov's Classical Conditioning
Definition

A form of associative learning in which a previously neutral stimulus, paired repeatedly with an unconditioned stimulus that elicits an unconditioned response, comes to elicit a conditioned response on its own.

Explanation

Ivan Pavlov (1849–1936) demonstrated this with dogs, food and a bell. Watson took the principle and applied it to humans, including in the Little Albert study. Later renamed "respondent conditioning" by Skinner to distinguish it from operant conditioning.

Example

Food (UCS) → salivation (UCR). Bell + food, repeated. Bell alone (CS) → salivation (CR).

Exam application

"Explain classical (respondent) conditioning and describe how Watson applied it."

Model answer: Classical conditioning involves the formation of an association between a neutral stimulus and an unconditioned stimulus, such that the previously neutral stimulus comes to elicit the response on its own. Watson applied the principle to fear acquisition in the Little Albert study, pairing a white rat (neutral) with a loud noise (UCS for fear) until the rat alone elicited the fear response.

03
Branch Three

Methodological Behaviourism — Watson

Watson took Thorndike and Pavlov, threw out introspection, and named the new paradigm. His behaviourism is called methodological because its rationale is methodological — objective observation is a better method for psychology than introspection.

If Branch 2 supplied the materials, Watson built the house. The next concepts move from his founding statement, through the mechanism (S–R bonds, habit, conditioning), to the dramatic Little Albert demonstration and the troubling extensions to child rearing.

14Methodological Behaviourism
Definition

The position, founded by Watson, that objective observation is a better method for understanding psychology than introspection — and accordingly that psychology should adopt the methods developed in animal psychology, where introspection is impossible.

Explanation

Crucially, the rationale is methodological, not philosophical. Watson does not deny mental events exist; he denies that introspection is a useful way to study them. This distinguishes his position from Skinner's later radical behaviourism. Founding statement: Watson's 1913 article "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It" in Psychological Review.

Example

Watson studying rats with stopwatches and mazes — the same techniques can, he argues, be applied to humans without needing introspective reports.

Exam application

"Define methodological behaviourism. Why is it called methodological?"

Model answer: Methodological behaviourism is the view that psychology should restrict itself to objective methods — those used in animal psychology — because introspection is unreliable. It is called methodological because its rationale concerns method (introspection doesn't work) rather than ontology (whether mental events exist). Watson's 1913 paper sets out the position: psychology is "a purely objective experimental branch of natural science" whose theoretical goal is the prediction and control of behaviour.

15The Stimulus–Response (S–R) Bond
Definition

A learned association between an environmental stimulus and a behavioural response that, when repeated, becomes a habit.

Explanation

The central explanatory unit of Watson's theory. Habits — accumulations of S–R bonds — were responsible for most behaviour. Conditioning could either build healthy habits (well-adjusted person) or pathological ones (neurotic person).

Example

Sight of a loud bar (S) → flinch (R). Repeated. Eventually the bar's appearance alone is sufficient to produce the flinch.

Exam application

"How does Watson's concept of the S–R bond relate to Thorndike's law of effect and James's concept of habit?"

Model answer: Watson's S–R bond synthesises both. From Thorndike's law of effect he takes the idea that connections between situation and response are strengthened by their consequences. From James he takes the idea of habit as the cumulative result of repetition. The S–R bond becomes Watson's central explanatory unit — the building block of all habitual behaviour.

16Watson's Environmentalism (the "Dozen Healthy Infants" Claim)
Definition

The view that environmental conditioning, given the right circumstances, can shape any healthy infant into any specialist — doctor, lawyer, artist, beggar, thief — regardless of inherited talents or ancestry.

Explanation

Watson's strongest formulation of the environmental determination of behaviour. Underlying assumption: humans (like animals) are adaptive systems that mould themselves to environmental demand.

Example

The famous quote (Watson, 1924/1970, p. 10): give him a dozen healthy infants and his own specified world and he could produce any specialist on demand.

Exam application

"What does Watson's 'dozen healthy infants' claim reveal about his theoretical commitments?"

Model answer: The claim reveals two commitments. First, a strongly environmentalist position — behaviour is determined by conditioning, not inheritance. Second, an underlying view of humans as adaptive systems that mould themselves to environmental demand, which is the same view he held of animals and which underwrites the assumption of animal–human continuity.

17The Little Albert Study (Watson & Rayner, 1920)
Definition

A demonstration that a fear response in a young child could be acquired through classical conditioning by pairing a previously neutral stimulus (a white rat) with a loud aversive noise.

Explanation

The signature applied demonstration of Watson's behaviourism. It linked Pavlovian conditioning to clinical phenomena (neuroses, phobias). It is also notorious — for ethical reasons, for the unreliability of subsequent retellings, and for the fact that the conditioning effect was relatively weak and required "freshening up".

Example

White rat presented → no fear. Rat + loud noise (steel bar struck with hammer) repeated. Five days after final pairing, rat alone presented → Albert cries and crawls away. Fear generalised to rabbit, dog, fur coat, cotton wool, Father Christmas.

Exam application

"Describe the Little Albert study and discuss two limitations of the work."

Model answer: Watson and Rayner conditioned a fear response in nine-month-old "Little Albert" by pairing a white rat with a loud noise made by striking a steel bar; the fear generalised to other furry stimuli. Limitations: (i) the conditioning effect was actually weak — Watson had to "freshen up" the response — and later textbook retellings invented objects (teddy bear, fur glove) not present in the original report; (ii) the study would not now obtain ethical approval, and the identity and health of "Albert" remain disputed (Beck et al. 2009 vs. Digdon et al. 2014), which raises questions about Watson's reporting accuracy.

18Generalisation of Conditioned Fear
Definition

The phenomenon whereby a conditioned response acquired to one stimulus is also elicited by similar stimuli that were never directly paired with the unconditioned stimulus.

Explanation

Significant for the Little Albert study because it showed conditioned fear was not narrowly tied to one particular object. This generalisation is precisely what makes a conditioning account of phobias plausible — phobias are typically about classes of stimulus, not single instances.

Example

Albert, conditioned to fear the white rat, also showed fear to a rabbit, a dog, a fur coat, cotton wool and a Father Christmas mask.

Exam application

"What is stimulus generalisation and why was it important in the Little Albert study?"

Model answer: Stimulus generalisation is the elicitation of a conditioned response by stimuli similar to, but not identical with, the originally conditioned stimulus. It mattered for Little Albert because it showed conditioning produced a transferable fear response — laying the groundwork for a behaviourist theory of phobias and clinical neuroses.

19Watson's Approach to Child Rearing
Definition

A set of recommendations, set out in Psychological Care of the Infant and Child (1928), based on the assumption that children are mini-adults to whom the same conditioning principles apply as to rats and adults.

Explanation

Some recommendations are uncontroversial (regular routines, prevent misbehaviour by keeping children busy, use punishment sparingly). The controversial element was the recommendation that children should not be shown affection — Watson believed it produced over-dependence. This is now considered entirely wrong: emotional attachment in early life contributes to later well-being (Bowlby, 1982).

Example

"Mothers just don't know, when they kiss their children… that they are slowly building up a human being totally unable to cope with the world it must later live in" (Watson, 1928/1972, p. 44).

Exam application

"Evaluate Watson's behaviourist approach to child rearing."

Model answer: Watson treated children as mini-adults, applying conditioning principles to upbringing. Some advice — regular routines, sparing punishment — remains broadly defensible. The advice to withhold affection, however, is now considered seriously wrong: attachment research from Bowlby onwards shows emotional attachment is essential for later well-being. The personal sequel is sobering: both Watson's sons experienced depression and one died by suicide, attributing their unhappiness to their upbringing.

04
Branch Four

Radical Behaviourism — Skinner

Watson rejected introspection on methodological grounds. Skinner went further. Drawing on philosophy of science — specifically Hempel's paradox of theorising — Skinner argued that all theoretical terms in psychology are redundant. Behaviourism, on his view, should restrict itself to S→R links irrespective of the nature of the intervening O.

The next concepts move from the philosophical foundations of Skinner's position (observation vs theoretical terms, the paradox of theorising, explanatory fictions) through to his constructive contributions (operant conditioning, the Skinner box, reinforcement) and the cultural reach of his ideas.

20Radical Behaviourism
Definition

Skinner's position that psychology should restrict itself to S–R links and ignore psychological theoretical terms irrespective of their nature — whether mentalistic or physiological.

Explanation

Distinct from Watson's methodological behaviourism in two ways. (i) The rationale is philosophical (theoretical terms are redundant) rather than methodological (introspection is unreliable). (ii) Skinner rejects all theoretical terms — physiological as well as mental — whereas Watson only rejected the mental.

Example

The S→O→R explanation "Lion → Fear → Running away" is rejected by Skinner. So is "Lion → Cortical activation → Running away." Both invoke an unobservable O variable.

Exam application

"How does Skinner's radical behaviourism differ from Watson's methodological behaviourism?"

Model answer: Watson's behaviourism is methodological — based on the unreliability of introspection — and rejects mental content while accepting that physiological explanation might be valid. Skinner's is radical because it rests on a philosophy-of-science argument (the paradox of theorising) and rejects all theoretical terms in psychology, mentalistic and physiological alike. The two positions reach similar empirical conclusions for very different reasons.

21Observation Terms vs Theoretical Terms
Definition

A distinction in philosophy of science. Observation terms refer to things that can be seen (tables, levers, dials). Theoretical terms refer to things that cannot (electrons, atoms, fear).

Explanation

For many explanations, theoretical terms intervene between observation terms — A (observation) causes B (observation) because of C (a theoretical-level rationale). In psychology this becomes Stimulus → Organism variable → Response (S → O → R). The crucial issue is whether the O variable is genuinely doing explanatory work or merely re-describing the connection.

Example

Lion (S) → Fear (O) → Running away (R). The O term "fear" is a theoretical term — not directly observable. Lion (S) → Cortical activation (O) → Running away (R) is the same form with a physiological O variable.

Exam application

"Explain the distinction between observation and theoretical terms and apply it to the psychological formula S → O → R."

Model answer: Observation terms refer to things that can be directly observed; theoretical terms refer to things that must be inferred. In psychology, S (stimulus) and R (response) are observation terms, while O (organism variable) is a theoretical term — it could be a mental state (fear), a physiological state (cortical activation), or any other inferred construct. Whether the O variable is doing genuine explanatory work, or is redundant given the S–R link, became a key issue between behaviourists and their successors.

22The Paradox of Theorising (Hempel, 1958)
Definition

An argument that theoretical terms are ultimately redundant: their purpose is to form links between observation terms, but once those links exist, the theoretical term is no longer needed because the observation terms can be connected directly.

Explanation

Skinner's philosophical foundation. If S → O → R, then once the connection is established the O drops out and S → R is sufficient. This argument, applied to psychology, motivated radical behaviourism's rejection of all theoretical terms.

Example

Once we know which lions cause running away, we no longer need "fear" to make the prediction.

Exam application

"State the paradox of theorising and explain its implications for psychology."

Model answer: The paradox, articulated by Hempel in 1958, holds that theoretical terms exist to link observation terms, but once those links are established the theoretical terms become redundant. For psychology, this means that if the organism variable in S → O → R does its job, the formula simplifies to S → R. Skinner used this argument to motivate radical behaviourism's rejection of theoretical terms. The neobehaviourist response was to allow theoretical terms only when they were operationally defined.

23Explanatory Fiction
Definition

A theoretical term that masquerades as an explanation but only re-describes the behaviour it is supposed to explain. The term is inferred from the behaviour and then used to explain that same behaviour.

Explanation

Skinner's central criticism of mentalistic and other organism-variable explanations. The illusion of explanation is provided when the theoretical term simply re-labels the observation. A later section of the chapter explains what is required to make an organism variable not an explanatory fiction.

Example

"John runs away because he is afraid." How do we know he is afraid? Because he runs away. Fear has been inferred from his behaviour and is now being used to explain it — circular, hence an explanatory fiction.

Exam application

"What is an explanatory fiction? Provide an example."

Model answer: An explanatory fiction is a theoretical term that appears to explain behaviour but in fact only re-describes it. The term is inferred from the very behaviour it is then used to explain — making the explanation circular. Example: John smiles, so we infer he is happy; we then claim he smiles because he is happy, even though our only evidence for happiness is his smiling.

24Operant vs Respondent (Classical) Conditioning
Definition

Operant conditioning: the shaping of voluntary behaviour through reinforcement of its consequences. Respondent conditioning: the form of conditioning studied by Pavlov (also called classical conditioning).

Explanation

Skinner's main academic contribution. Operant conditioning is directly linked to Thorndike's law of effect: organisms do more of an action that is reinforced and less of one that is not. Skinner avoided the explanatory-fiction problem of "satisfaction" by simply identifying the conditions that were reinforcing — defining reinforcement by its effect on response rate.

Example

Rat presses lever (R), receives food pellet. Lever-press rate increases. Operant. Vs. Pavlov's bell + salivation, which is respondent.

Exam application

"Distinguish operant and respondent conditioning, and explain how Skinner avoided the problem of explanatory fictions."

Model answer: Respondent conditioning, studied by Pavlov, involves the elicitation of an automatic response by a previously neutral stimulus paired with an unconditioned stimulus. Operant conditioning, established by Skinner, involves the shaping of voluntary behaviour by its consequences. Skinner avoided the circularity of Thorndike's "satisfaction" by simply identifying the conditions that were reinforcing — defining reinforcement operationally as whatever increased the rate of a particular response.

25The Skinner Box
Definition

An experimental apparatus consisting of a box in which a rat is placed with a lever. Lever presses constitute the response. Stimuli — food pellets, electric shocks — can be applied by the experimenter.

Explanation

The technological core of Skinner's research programme. It allowed precise measurement of response rate as a function of reinforcement schedule, generating an immense amount of data on reinforcement schedules and their effects.

Example

A rat in a Skinner box on a variable-ratio reinforcement schedule will press the lever rapidly and persistently — the principle behind slot machines.

Exam application

"Describe the Skinner box and explain its methodological contribution."

Model answer: The Skinner box is a controlled environment in which a single response (lever-press) can be measured precisely as a function of stimuli applied by the experimenter. Methodologically, it allowed Skinner to study the effects of reinforcement schedules under conditions of strict experimental control, generating quantitative data on response rates that supported the operant-conditioning framework.

26Positive vs Negative Reinforcement
Definition

Positive reinforcement: gaining a positive experience, which increases the rate of the behaviour. Negative reinforcement: avoiding a negative experience, which also increases the rate of the behaviour.

Explanation

Both increase behaviour — the difference is in the type of consequence. Skinner believed reinforcement was more effective at shaping behaviour than punishment, and accordingly advocated encouragement over punishment. Walden Two describes a positive-reinforcement utopia.

Example

Positive: studying because you enjoy learning. Negative: studying to avoid being beaten. Both increase studying — the experience differs.

Exam application

"Distinguish positive and negative reinforcement. What was Skinner's view of their relative value?"

Model answer: Positive reinforcement increases behaviour by adding a positive consequence; negative reinforcement increases behaviour by removing or avoiding a negative consequence. Both increase behaviour. Skinner held that positive reinforcement was a more effective and more humane control technique than punishment, and advocated its use in education, child rearing and the design of social institutions.

27Walden Two & Beyond Freedom and Dignity
Definition

Skinner's two best-known popular books. Walden Two (1948) describes a utopia organised around positive reinforcement. Beyond Freedom and Dignity (1971) argues that freedom and dignity are illusions because all behaviour is environmentally controlled.

Explanation

The argument: if you do things for positive reinforcement you call it freedom; if you do things to avoid punishment or for negative reinforcement, you call it control. Either way, your behaviour is controlled. Aldous Huxley's Brave New World (1932) presents the same idea satirically, exposing the emptiness of a happy life controlled by others.

Example

You write your essays out of positive reinforcement (interest, the prospect of a good grade) and so feel free. If forced to write essays under threat of being beaten, you would feel coerced. Same behaviour, same control — different feeling.

Exam application

"Outline Skinner's argument that freedom is an illusion."

Model answer: Skinner argues in Beyond Freedom and Dignity that all behaviour is shaped by reinforcement contingencies. When the contingencies are positive, we experience the behaviour as freely chosen; when they are aversive (punishment or negative reinforcement), we experience the same behaviour as coerced. Either way the behaviour is controlled, so the sense of freedom is the illusion of being controlled by positive consequences. Huxley's Brave New World presents this argument satirically by depicting a happiness-controlled society that is nonetheless empty.

28Skinner's Practical Inventions (Aircrib, Teaching Machine, Summator)
Definition

Three applications of behaviourist principles. The aircrib (originally "baby tender") was a controlled-environment crib for infants. Teaching machines individualised the rate of learning and provided immediate feedback. The summator (or tautophone) was an auditory inkblot for projective testing.

Explanation

Skinner believed behaviourist principles could increase human happiness. The applied work was a key part of his programme but, in his own view, "not particularly successful". Public unease — the aircrib was viewed as a "baby cage" rather than a luxury crib — limited adoption.

Example

Skinner's daughter Deborah used the aircrib for her first two years and grew up to be a happy and successful adult. About 50 people who used aircribs as babies were later tracked, with no evidence of harm.

Exam application

"What were Skinner's main applied contributions, and why were they not more successful?"

Model answer: Skinner's main applied contributions were the aircrib (controlled-environment crib), teaching machines (individualised instruction with immediate feedback), and the summator (auditory inkblot). The reasons for their limited success include cultural mismatch — by the 1960s the public was moving away from a mechanistic view of people toward a more liberal one — and image problems, such as the aircrib being received as a "baby cage". The principles themselves were technically sound but did not achieve cultural traction.

05
Branch Five

Neobehaviourism — Hull & Tolman

Skinner's strict S–R discipline was theoretically uneconomical when prior history mattered: a long list of historical stimuli is unwieldy. Neobehaviourists allowed theoretical terms back in, on one strict condition: they must be operationally defined. Within that constraint, Hull built an atomistic mathematical theory and Tolman a molar, purposive one.

The next concepts move from the shared rule of operational definition through Hull's quantitative atomism (sER, drive, habit) to Tolman's molar, purposive, S–S learning — the figure who already begins to anticipate cognitive psychology.

29Neobehaviourism
Definition

A form of behaviourism that allows organism variables into the explanatory framework, but only when those variables have been operationally defined.

Explanation

Motivated by the impracticality of the strict radical-behaviourist solution to historical effects on behaviour. Rather than listing every prior stimulus, the neobehaviourist allows a single organism variable that summarises external history, provided that variable is precisely defined by its measurement procedures.

Example

Instead of listing 200 prior occasions on which a rat was fed in the goal box, Hull defines "habit" as a function of those occasions and uses habit as the explanatory variable.

Exam application

"Why did neobehaviourism arise, and what was its central methodological commitment?"

Model answer: Neobehaviourism arose because radical behaviourism's strict S–R approach became unwieldy when behaviour depended on a long history of prior stimuli. Its central methodological commitment was to operational definition: organism variables were permitted only when defined precisely in terms of their measurement operations or observable effects.

30Operational Definition
Definition

A definition of a concept in terms of its operations — that is, in terms of what it does, the observable effects it produces, or the observable events that produce it.

Explanation

The methodological gatekeeper of neobehaviourism. An operationally defined organism variable is precisely defined and grounded in observation. The classic example: Intelligence is what intelligence tests measure. The implication, important for Branch 6, is that an operationally defined term has no existence independent of its definition.

Example

Drive (D) operationally defined as the number of hours a rat has been deprived of food. Habit (H) operationally defined as the number of times the rat has performed the behaviour in the past.

Exam application

"What is an operational definition? Provide an example from neobehaviourism."

Model answer: An operational definition specifies a concept entirely in terms of the operations used to measure or produce it. In Hull's neobehaviourism, drive is operationally defined as hours of food deprivation and habit as the number of past occasions on which the response has occurred. The crucial consequence is that, for an operationally defined variable, there is nothing more to the concept than its measurement operations.

31Hull's Mathematical Theory (sER = D × H)
Definition

A quantitative theory in which Net Reaction Potential (sER) — the potential to engage in a particular behaviour — is given by the product of Drive (D) and Habit (H): sER = D × H.

Explanation

Hull (1884–1952) attempted to mimic the mathematical theories of physics and chemistry. The metaphor: drive energises behaviour, habit directs it. Each variable is operationally defined. Hull is one of the few psychologists to have introduced quantitative theory in this way; the Journal of Mathematical Psychology (1964–) continues this tradition.

Example

A rat deprived of food for 12 hours (high D) that has run a maze 200 times (high H) will run the maze faster than a satiated rat (low D) that has run it twice (low H).

Exam application

"State Hull's basic equation and explain what each term means."

Model answer: Hull proposed sER = D × H, where sER is net reaction potential (approximately equal to behaviour), D is drive (operationally defined, e.g., as hours of food deprivation), and H is habit (operationally defined, e.g., as the number of times the response has been performed). Drive energises behaviour while habit directs it; both are operationally defined and so consistent with neobehaviourist constraints.

32Atomistic Theorising
Definition

Theorising in which the determinants of behaviour are simply added together — the whole is no more than the sum of its parts.

Explanation

Hull's approach. As his laboratory set-ups became more complex, more organism variables had to be added: incentive (K), work (W), and others. The equation became more complex. The atomistic strategy meant that complexity scaled additively, eventually producing a theory too unwieldy to simplify the world. Contrast with Tolman, who came from the gestalt tradition (wholes before parts).

Example

An expanded Hull-style equation: sER = (D × H) + K + W + … Each new finding added a new variable.

Exam application

"Explain the term 'atomistic theorising' and discuss why it caused difficulty for Hull's theory."

Model answer: Atomistic theorising treats the determinants of behaviour as additive: behaviour equals the sum of its parts. Hull's theory was atomistic, so each new experimental finding required a new variable to be added to the equation. This caused difficulty because the theory grew progressively more unwieldy without becoming any better at simplifying behaviour — the function a theory is supposed to perform.

33Degenerating Problem Shift (Lakatos)
Definition

A research programme in which theory lags behind data — new findings drive new ad hoc theoretical concepts rather than the theory generating new predictions.

Explanation

Lakatos contrasted theory-led with data-led research, arguing the latter is less successful. Hull's theory is the textbook's example: each unexplained behaviour led to a new theoretical concept rather than a more powerful theory. The chapter's broader claim is that behaviourism as a whole was data-driven, which contributed to its eventual exhaustion.

Example

An effect of incentive size emerges in the data, so K is added. An effect of effort emerges, so W is added. The theory inflates rather than deepening.

Exam application

"What is a degenerating problem shift, and how does Hull's theory exemplify it?"

Model answer: A degenerating problem shift, in Lakatos's framework, is research in which theory lags behind data — new findings provoke new theoretical concepts rather than being predicted by the existing theory. Hull's theory is an example: as new variables affecting maze running emerged, new concepts were added to the equation, producing a progressively more complex theory that did not improve predictive simplification of behaviour.

34Tolman's Purposive (Molar) Behaviourism
Definition

A form of neobehaviourism that treats behaviour as a molar event — an act with a goal — rather than a sequence of fine-grained actions.

Explanation

Tolman (1886–1959) was influenced by gestalt psychologists Koffka and Lewin (he worked with Koffka), and by Köhler's insight learning. His behaviourism is purposive because acts are defined by their goals — "arriving at the goal box" rather than "step left, step left, step right". This makes Tolman a transition figure between behaviourism and cognitive psychology.

Example

For Hull, a rat's behaviour in a maze is a sequence of left/right/forward actions. For Tolman, it is the act of getting to the goal box.

Exam application

"Why is Tolman's behaviourism described as 'purposive' and 'molar'?"

Model answer: It is molar because Tolman treats behaviour as a large unit — an act — rather than the smaller actions Hull used. It is purposive because acts are defined in terms of their goal: an act is the arriving at the goal box, not the individual movements that achieve it. The molar/purposive emphasis owes to Tolman's gestalt influences (Koffka, Lewin) and anticipates cognitive psychology.

35Acts vs Actions
Definition

Tolman's distinction. An action is a small, specific movement (a step left). An act is a larger, goal-defined unit (arriving at the goal box).

Explanation

This is the unit-of-analysis disagreement between Tolman and Hull. Hull says the action is what is reinforced; Tolman says it is the act. The choice of unit determines what kind of theory you can build — fine-grained associationism (Hull) or holistic, goal-directed psychology (Tolman).

Example

Reinforcing the rat for "two steps forward, three left, four forward" (Hull's actions) vs reinforcing it for "getting to the food" (Tolman's act).

Exam application

"Distinguish acts and actions and explain why the distinction mattered."

Model answer: Actions are the small, specific movements that make up behaviour; acts are larger, goal-defined units. The distinction mattered because it determined what counted as the unit of reinforcement: for Hull, individual actions; for Tolman, whole acts defined by their goals. The choice committed each theorist to a different kind of psychology — atomistic associationism for Hull, purposive holism for Tolman.

36Cognitive Map & S–S Bonds
Definition

Tolman's proposal that, in a maze, rats form a mental representation of the environment — a cognitive map — based on stimulus–stimulus (S–S) bonds, which they then use in later behaviour.

Explanation

The most explicit anticipation of cognitive psychology in the chapter. The very term cognitive map is now part of cognitive vocabulary. Where Hull's rats made gradual S–R bonds, Tolman's formed S–S bonds and went straight to the goal once the map was acquired.

Example

A rat placed in a maze first explores. Once it has formed the cognitive map, it goes directly to the goal box on subsequent trials.

Exam application

"What is a cognitive map, and why is the term significant in the history of psychology?"

Model answer: A cognitive map is a mental representation of an environment, formed from S–S bonds, that guides later behaviour. The term is significant because it was introduced by a behaviourist (Tolman) and yet is unambiguously cognitive — it refers to an internal representation. It marks Tolman as a transition figure and demonstrates that behaviourism was not monolithic in its rejection of mental concepts.

37All-or-Nothing vs Incremental Learning
Definition

Two competing accounts of how learning happens. All-or-nothing: the animal either knows or does not know — there is no point in between (Tolman, gestalt tradition). Incremental: habits are gradually strengthened by repetition (Hull, Wundtian associationism).

Explanation

A major debate of the period. Köhler had shown apes could learn through insight (see Chapter 10), supporting Tolman. Hull's rats made gradual progress, supporting Hull. The eventual resolution: the two camps were using different rat strains. Hull's were selectively bred to be unemotional; Tolman's were closer to wild rats — more cautious and so only went to the goal once they had mapped the maze.

Example

Köhler's chimpanzees suddenly seeing the solution (insight). Hull's rats progressively reducing errors (gradual).

Exam application

"Outline the all-or-nothing vs incremental learning debate. How was it eventually resolved?"

Model answer: Tolman (gestalt-influenced) argued learning is all-or-nothing — the animal either has the cognitive map or does not. Hull (associationist) argued learning is incremental — habits are gradually strengthened by repetition. The debate was eventually resolved by the recognition that the two laboratories were using different rat strains: Hull's were unemotional and produced gradual learning curves, whereas Tolman's were closer to wild rats, more cautious, and so produced all-or-nothing learning once they had mapped the environment.

06
Branch Six

Hypothetical Constructs & Intervening Variables

A 1948 paper by MacCorquodale & Meehl made the distinction that finally allowed psychology to leave behind both Skinner's strict S–R and Hull's atomism. Some organism variables are merely shorthand summaries of measurement (intervening variables); others are hypothesised to exist independently (hypothetical constructs). The latter is what cognitive psychology would adopt.

This is the philosophical hinge of the chapter. The next four concepts work through the central distinction (intervening variable vs hypothetical construct), the ontological status that distinguishes them, the surplus meaning that hypothetical constructs carry, and the consequence: the rise of the person variable.

38Hypothetical Constructs vs Intervening Variables (MacCorquodale & Meehl, 1948)
Definition

An intervening variable is an organism variable defined entirely by its measurement procedures — there is nothing more to it than its operations. A hypothetical construct is hypothesised to exist independently of measurement and so retains some existence beyond its definition.

Explanation

The decisive theoretical move of the chapter. The distinction enabled the ontological re-acceptance of mental terms in psychology — provided one was willing to treat them as hypothetical constructs (existing in some sense in the person) rather than as mere intervening variables.

Example

"Intelligence is what intelligence tests measure" — intervening variable. "Intelligence tests measure intelligence" — hypothetical construct (intelligence is presupposed to exist independently of the test).

Exam application

"Explain the distinction between hypothetical constructs and intervening variables, using intelligence as the example."

Model answer: An intervening variable is an organism variable defined wholly by its measurement operations — for example, "intelligence is what intelligence tests measure" treats intelligence as nothing more than the test's output. A hypothetical construct, by contrast, is hypothesised to exist independently of measurement: "intelligence tests measure intelligence" presupposes that intelligence exists prior to the test, which is therefore an imperfect estimate. The distinction matters because hypothetical constructs have ontological status — they are genuine entities — whereas intervening variables do not.

39Ontological Status
Definition

The technical term for the assumption that a hypothetical construct exists as an entity, independently of the procedures used to measure it.

Explanation

Where intervening variables have no ontological status (there is nothing there other than the measurement), hypothetical constructs do — there is "something there", though exactly what it is can be disputed. This is the key conceptual upgrade that distinguishes neobehaviourist organism variables from cognitive person variables.

Example

Memory has ontological status because it is presupposed to exist in the person whether or not we measure it. A psychologist's "drive" defined as hours of food deprivation does not.

Exam application

"Define ontological status. Why is it important in distinguishing hypothetical constructs from intervening variables?"

Model answer: Ontological status is the assumption that a construct refers to an entity that exists independently of how we measure it. Hypothetical constructs have ontological status — there is "something there" beyond the measurement procedures. Intervening variables do not — they are mere shorthand for the measurement. The distinction is crucial because cognitive psychology adopts hypothetical constructs (memory, attention, schema) as entities that genuinely exist in the person and influence behaviour.

40Surplus Meaning
Definition

The properties of a hypothetical construct that are not exhausted by what it directly does — additional features that make the construct fertile for generating new predictions.

Explanation

An intervening variable is precisely defined by its operations and has no surplus. A hypothetical construct is approximately defined and may motivate things other than its primary effect. Surplus meaning therefore acts as a heuristic — a guide to future research and new empirical predictions.

Example

If achievement motivation motivates achievement, the surplus meaning is that it may also motivate other achievement-relevant phenomena — writing more stories about achievement, for example.

Exam application

"What is surplus meaning, and why is it valuable for psychological theorising?"

Model answer: Surplus meaning refers to the properties of a hypothetical construct that go beyond the construct's directly measured effects. It is valuable because it functions as a heuristic for future research: a construct hypothesised to motivate achievement might be predicted to influence achievement-related phenomena that have not yet been measured. Intervening variables, being precisely and completely defined by their operations, generate no surplus and therefore no new predictions.

41Person Variable
Definition

The cognitive paradigm's name for a hypothetical construct — an organism variable that is hypothesised to exist in the person and is not merely a summary of external observations.

Explanation

The terminological hinge between the chapter's behaviourist and cognitive halves. Where neobehaviourism would have called intelligence an "organism variable", cognitive psychology calls it a "person variable" — and the change of name carries the change of status (ontological, surplus-bearing).

Example

Beliefs, attitudes, expectancies, schemas, achievement motives — all are person variables.

Exam application

"What is a person variable, and how does it differ from a neobehaviourist organism variable?"

Model answer: A person variable is the cognitive paradigm's term for a hypothetical construct — an entity hypothesised to exist in the person, with ontological status and surplus meaning. It differs from a neobehaviourist organism variable in that the latter is typically operationally defined and treated as an intervening variable: a shorthand for measurement with no independent existence. The person variable thus reintroduces mental content into psychology, but in a way that is consistent with hypothetical-construct logic.

07
Branch Seven

The Cognitive Turn

Behaviourism did not collapse — it was overtaken. The Skinner–Chomsky debate is widely (over-)credited with the change. The deeper causes were that behaviourism ran out of steam (a degenerating problem shift on the grand scale), while cultural and technological change — the move from production lines to computers — produced a new set of questions that information-processing theory could answer.

The final concepts trace the conditions under which the cognitive paradigm replaced behaviourism: a famous theoretical controversy, two systemic causes, the new vocabulary of "model", and a wider notion of cognition that quietly reabsorbed the question of free will.

42The Skinner–Chomsky Debate
Definition

A controversy over the explanation of language. Skinner's Verbal Behavior (1957) attempted to account for human language using behavioural principles. Chomsky's 1959 review argued that language depends on an innate language acquisition device.

Explanation

Often credited with heralding the demise of behaviourism — though the chapter argues this is almost certainly an oversimplification. Skinner's view was less simplistic than typically caricatured. The deeper problem with Skinner's account was not that it failed to explain language in humans but that it could not explain why other animals, given comparable language exposure (e.g. household dogs), do not develop language.

Example

Chomsky's challenge: a dog raised in a household with constant linguistic exposure does not learn to speak. If language were merely associative learning, why not?

Exam application

"Outline the Skinner–Chomsky debate. How decisive was it for the demise of behaviourism?"

Model answer: Skinner's Verbal Behavior applied behavioural principles to human language; Chomsky's review argued that language relies on an innate language acquisition device. The debate is widely credited with heralding behaviourism's demise, but this is almost certainly an oversimplification. Skinner's position was more sophisticated than usually portrayed, and the chief difficulty with the behavioural account was not that it failed to explain language in humans but that it could not explain why other species, given similar exposure, did not acquire it. Behaviourism more plausibly declined for systemic reasons — running out of steam and being overtaken by information-processing theory.

43"Running Out of Steam"
Definition

The chapter's term for the systemic exhaustion of behaviourism: once the behaviour of the rat in T-mazes and Skinner boxes had been thoroughly explored, there was little theoretically left to discover.

Explanation

Behaviourism was data-driven (not theory-led). Its principal ideas — classical conditioning, operant conditioning and their extensions — had been worked out. The findings remained correct; they simply ceased to generate new theoretical questions. This is the macro-scale analogue of a degenerating problem shift.

Example

By the late 1950s, the basic curves of reinforcement schedules had been mapped. Further data added detail rather than insight.

Exam application

"What is meant by behaviourism 'running out of steam', and why is this relevant to its decline?"

Model answer: The phrase captures the idea that, by the late 1950s, the principal phenomena studied by behaviourism — classical and operant conditioning in T-mazes and Skinner boxes — had been thoroughly mapped. Because behaviourism was data-driven rather than theory-led, the diminishing return on new findings translated directly into theoretical exhaustion. The remaining behaviourist results stayed valid; they simply stopped generating new questions, and a new paradigm — cognitive psychology, with new questions — took over.

44Cultural & Technological Factors (Production Lines vs Computers)
Definition

The argument that paradigms in psychology coincide with the dominant technologies of their time. Behaviourism coincided with factory production lines (simple repetitive movements). Cognitive psychology coincided with the computer (information processing).

Explanation

A surprisingly powerful explanation of why one paradigm replaces another. The questions a paradigm can plausibly ask are constrained by the technological metaphors available. Slot machines, the chapter notes, are still a fine demonstration of variable-ratio reinforcement — but they are not the dominant technology of the modern world.

Example

The factory worker on a production line performs simple, reinforced repetitive movements — fits the behaviourist frame. The office worker using a computer manipulates information — fits the cognitive frame.

Exam application

"What role did cultural and technological change play in the decline of behaviourism?"

Model answer: Behaviourism developed alongside factory production lines, where simple repetitive movements were the dominant form of work — a context in which behaviourist principles applied naturally. The development of computers in the 1950s and 1960s introduced a new metaphor: information processing. Computers store, transform and combine information that cannot be represented in S–R bonds. The new metaphor produced new questions — about memory, problem-solving and attention — which behaviourism could not address, and which became the subject matter of cognitive psychology.

45Psychological Model
Definition

A theory whose form mirrors that of an external system (typically a piece of technology), even though its content is psychological. A term rarely used before 1950 and central to cognitive psychology.

Explanation

A model car has the same form as a real car (four wheels, bonnet, boot) but different content. A psychological model has the same form as some external system — a thermostat, a control system, a digital computer — but the content is psychological. The cognitive paradigm is defined, in part, by its use of technology as a heuristic for theory.

Example

The control-system model of motivation: goal → decision → motivation → behaviour → environment → sensory input → decision (Figure 4.1). The form is a thermostat; the content is psychological.

Exam application

"What is meant by 'psychological model', and what role do models play in cognitive psychology?"

Model answer: A psychological model is a theory whose form mirrors that of an external (often technological) system, while its content concerns psychological phenomena. A control-system model of motivation, for example, has the same structure as a thermostat — feedback loops, error signals, comparators — but the variables are goals, decisions, motivation and behaviour. Models matter because they give cognitive psychology a heuristic for theory construction: technology supplies the form, while psychology supplies the content.

46The Cognitive Paradigm Formula (Situation → Person → Behaviour)
Definition

The cognitive paradigm's reformulation of the neobehaviourist S → O → R, in which Stimulus becomes Situation or Environment, Organism becomes Person, and Response becomes Behaviour.

Explanation

The change of names reflects the change of paradigm. "Situation" widens the input beyond a single stimulus; "Person" implies a being with internal states (hypothetical constructs); "Behaviour" widens the output beyond a single response. The structural form is preserved — cognitive psychology builds on rather than supplants behaviourism.

Example

An exam (situation) → student's anxiety, beliefs about ability, motivation (person variables) → exam performance (behaviour).

Exam application

"How is the neobehaviourist formula S → O → R transformed in the cognitive paradigm?"

Model answer: S → O → R becomes Situation/Environment → Person → Behaviour. Stimulus is generalised to situation, broadening the input. Organism is replaced by Person, signalling that the intervening variables are person variables — hypothetical constructs with ontological status, not just intervening variables. Response is generalised to behaviour, broadening the output. The structural form is retained, illustrating that the cognitive paradigm builds on, rather than discards, the behaviourist framework.

47Broadbent's Perception and Communication (1958)
Definition

A foundational text of cognitive psychology, published before the Skinner–Chomsky debate, that explicitly applied information-processing principles from computers to the analysis of attention, memory and learning.

Explanation

The chapter cites Broadbent's chapter titles as evidence of how early the cognitive paradigm was already in motion: "Selective listening to speech," "Immediate memory and the shifting of attention," "The selective nature of learning". The book's pre-1959 publication date supports the chapter's claim that the Skinner–Chomsky debate was not as decisive as commonly thought.

Example

Broadbent's filter model of attention — a piece of cognitive theory built on a technological (filter) metaphor.

Exam application

"Why is Broadbent's Perception and Communication (1958) important for the history of the cognitive paradigm?"

Model answer: Broadbent's book is important for two reasons. First, it explicitly applies computer-style information-processing concepts to attention, memory and learning, supplying the new vocabulary of the cognitive paradigm. Second, its publication date — 1958, before the Skinner–Chomsky exchange — suggests that the cognitive paradigm was already developing on independent grounds. The Skinner–Chomsky debate, often given decisive weight, is therefore better seen as one moment in a wider intellectual transition.

48The Wider Cognitive Paradigm
Definition

The general use of the term cognitive to describe any explanation that relies on hypothetical constructs or person variables linked in some way to thought — including beliefs, attitudes, expectancies, and implicit cognition.

Explanation

Originally tied to thought, memory and reasoning, the term cognitive came to be used more broadly. Beliefs and attitudes are conscious and introspectable, but person variables can also be implicit — accessible to influence behaviour without being available to consciousness. The cognitive paradigm therefore acknowledges Würzburg's evidence (not all behaviour is consciously controlled) while still allowing mental content to do explanatory work.

Example

Implicit attitudes that influence behaviour without the person being able to report them — a possibility entirely consistent with the wider cognitive paradigm.

Exam application

"In what sense does the cognitive paradigm 'get the best of both worlds'?"

Model answer: The cognitive paradigm acknowledges Würzburg-style evidence that not all behaviour is consciously controlled, while still permitting mental content to explain behaviour. It does this by allowing both conscious and introspectable person variables (beliefs, attitudes) and implicit person variables not available to consciousness. The paradigm therefore captures the role of consciousness in some behaviour while not committing to consciousness as the sole or even primary determinant — which is what gives it explanatory range across social, developmental and clinical psychology.

49Free Will, Determinism & the Methodology of Science
Definition

The chapter's reflective coda. To explain a behaviour, an explanation must include the factors responsible for it — and the methodology of science is one of determinism. The existence of free will is unfalsifiable and therefore outside the realm of science.

Explanation

This is the philosophical residue of Skinner's argument. Whether or not behaviour is in fact deterministic, science as a methodology assumes determinism — psychology's silence on free will follows from this methodological commitment, not from a settled metaphysical answer.

Example

You may experience choosing to read this section as a free choice. Psychology, as a science, will look for the factors that influenced that choice — without thereby claiming that the experience of freedom is wrong.

Exam application

"Why does psychology generally not address the question of free will?"

Model answer: To explain a behaviour, an explanation must specify the factors that produced it — and science as a methodology assumes determinism. The existence of free will is unfalsifiable: there is no observation that could demonstrate a behaviour was uncaused. It therefore lies outside the methodological remit of science. Skinner's view that freedom is an illusion is consistent with this methodological position, although a deterministic methodology does not, by itself, settle the metaphysical question of whether free will exists.

50Beyond the Cognitive Paradigm — Co-existing Paradigms
Definition

The closing observation that no paradigm in psychology has ever been so dominant that it excluded everything else. Cognitive neuroscience, AI-based paradigms, person-uniqueness paradigms and qualitative paradigms all currently co-exist with the cognitive paradigm.

Explanation

An important corrective to a tidy succession story. Behaviourism was dominant; cognitive psychology became dominant; cognitive neuroscience now has the highest status. But each newly dominant paradigm has been accompanied by older ones that still produce useful work.

Example

Cognitive neuroscience studies the biology of cognitive concepts (Chapter 6). The qualitative paradigm operates with very different assumptions (Chapter 9). They are concurrent, not successive.

Exam application

"Is the history of psychology a history of paradigm replacement or of paradigm coexistence?"

Model answer: It is both. The chapter's narrative arc is one of replacement — introspectionism replaced by behaviourism, behaviourism replaced by the cognitive paradigm. But within any given period, multiple paradigms have co-existed, and several continue to do so today: cognitive neuroscience, AI-based approaches, paradigms emphasising the uniqueness of persons and qualitative paradigms. Dominance is therefore a matter of relative status rather than exclusion.

§ 2 · Common Student Mistakes

Where students typically go wrong

Each card identifies the misconception, explains why it happens, and gives the correct understanding.

i.

Conflating Watson and Skinner

Why it happens

Both reject mental content; both work with rats; both are called behaviourists. Students assume the rationale is the same.

Correct understanding

Watson rejected introspection on methodological grounds (it is unreliable). Skinner rejected all theoretical terms — mental and physiological — on philosophical grounds (the paradox of theorising). Watson would accept a physiological explanation; Skinner would not.

ii.

Reading the law of effect as obvious

Why it happens

"Animals do more of what they enjoy" sounds trivial — and the textbook itself jokes about it.

Correct understanding

The novelty was not the idea but the framing: psychology now had laws expressed in scientific form. The law also exposes a problem — "satisfaction" is defined circularly, an explanatory fiction. This circularity is exactly what Skinner later avoided by defining reinforcement by its effect on response rate.

iii.

Assuming Little Albert was a clean demonstration

Why it happens

Textbooks repeat the story so often it acquires the status of myth. Furry gloves and teddy bears get added in retellings.

Correct understanding

The conditioning effect was weak and required "freshening up". Subsequent retellings invented objects (rug, fur glove, teddy bear) not present in the 1920 paper. Albert's identity is contested. The study would not now obtain ethical approval. It illustrates conditioning and the unreliability of how histories of psychology are constructed.

iv.

Confusing operational definition with explanation

Why it happens

Operational definitions sound rigorous, so students assume they explain. They define.

Correct understanding

An operationally defined organism variable is a shorthand summary of external observations — it is an intervening variable, not a hypothetical construct. It does not refer to an entity that exists independently. Hull's "drive" defined as hours of food deprivation is a label, not an entity in the rat's body.

v.

Treating hypothetical constructs and intervening variables as interchangeable

Why it happens

Both are organism variables that intervene between stimulus and response. The terminology suggests overlap.

Correct understanding

Intervening variables are exhausted by their measurement; hypothetical constructs are not. Compare "intelligence is what intelligence tests measure" (intervening variable — no surplus, no ontological status) with "intelligence tests measure intelligence" (hypothetical construct — surplus meaning, ontological status). The cognitive paradigm uses the second sense.

vi.

Believing Chomsky's review killed behaviourism

Why it happens

It is a tidy story: Chomsky reviewed Verbal Behavior, behaviourism died.

Correct understanding

Skinner's view was less simplistic than usually portrayed. The deeper problem was that animal models could not explain why species given comparable language exposure (e.g. household dogs) did not develop language. Behaviourism's decline owed at least as much to running out of steam and to cultural-technological change as to one famous review.

vii.

Hearing "molar" and "molecular" as a value judgement

Why it happens

"Molar" sounds bigger and therefore better; students conclude Tolman is more sophisticated than Hull.

Correct understanding

Molar and molecular are unit-of-analysis choices. Tolman analyses behaviour in terms of acts (goal-defined wholes); Hull analyses it in terms of actions (component movements). Each choice is suited to particular research questions. The historical irony is that Tolman's gestalt-influenced choice anticipated cognitive psychology, but that does not make Hull's atomism wrong in principle — it lost on tractability.

viii.

Treating "model" as a synonym for "diagram"

Why it happens

Cognitive psychology is full of box-and-arrow diagrams labelled "models".

Correct understanding

A psychological model is a theory whose form mirrors that of an external system (often technological), while its content is psychological. The control-system model of motivation has the form of a thermostat but the content of motivation. The technology supplies a heuristic; the psychology supplies the actual phenomena.
